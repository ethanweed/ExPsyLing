History of Psycholinguistics

Early History of Psycholinguistics
(Notes from Levelt’s A History of Psycholinguistics)
Wilhelm von Humboldt (1767-1835)
Philosophy of State
Philosophy of Education
Diplomat
Linguist
Studied Basque
When he died, he was working on a book about the Kawi language in Java
View of language
Language is an activity - language is something we do
This is very different than early views that saw language as e.g. something that was written down, or as a spoken product, like an epic poem
Humboldt also mentions the existence of “inner speech”, but writes very little about it
Has early ideas about linguistic relativity, but not in the strong Whorfian sense - he talks more about certain language having certain affordances, but says that any idea can be expressed in any language
Johann Friedrich Herbert (1776-1841)
Perhaps the first psychologist?
The concept of an “idea”
Psychology is our inner life
Part of that inner life is conscious, the other part is our unconscious
Ideas inter our consciousness through our senses or through memories
Ideas are always in motion, and interact with each other like billiard balls (mental mechanics)
Consciousness is like a stage where ideas appear, interact, and then disappear offstage
When ideas become associated with each other, Herbert called this “apperception” (we will return to this with Wundt)
Heymann Steinthal (1823-1855)
Inspired by Herbert
Only one idea can be “onstage” at a time
Through interaction, ideas can gain “energy” and it is this energy that pushes them onto the stage of consciousness
Ideas can interact in different ways
Fusion: I see my cat, then later I see him again. The first image I have fuses with the later images I have, and they become one: my abstract idea of my cat
Entanglement: Ideas can become connected to each other, but still separate. My idea of “my cat” can become entangled with my idea of “animal”, and also “pet”
Max Müller (1823-1900)
Studied Sanskrit
He took the 1100 Sanskrit roots that Panini had written down two thousand years earlier and reduced them to 121 core concepts. “These 121 concepts constitute the stock-in-trade with which I maintain that every thought that has passed through the mind of India, so far as it is known to us in its literature, has been expressed.” (Levelt pg. 32)
“Language is the true autobiography of mind.” (quoted in Levelt, pg. 32)
Wilhelm Wundt (1832-1920)
Medical doctor - physiology
“Contributions to the Theory of Sense Perception” (1858–1862)
“Lectures on Human and Animal Psychology” (1863–1864)
“Principles of Physiological Psychology” (1874)
Laboratory of experimental psychology
Timing - clocks, electronic
Visitors to the lab included
Edward Sapir
Benjamin Lee Whorf
Edmund Husserel (phenomenology)
Franz Boas (anthropology)
Wundt’s “apperception”
Redefinition of Herbert’s word
“The state, characterised by a specific sensation, which accompanies the clearer focusing of some mental content we call attention; the specific process by which some mental content is brought into clear focus [we call] apperception”.
Levelt: Wundt’s “apperception” is very similar to the modern idea of “executive control”
